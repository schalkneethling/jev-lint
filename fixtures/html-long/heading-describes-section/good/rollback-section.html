Deploying safely

Rolling back a failed migration

“The worst outage I have been part of was not caused by the change itself. It was caused by four people typing into the same terminal at two in the morning, each of them certain that the others had already checked the thing they were about to skip.”

Priya Raman, staff engineer, speaking at a post-incident review in March

Before we get into any of the mechanics, it is worth saying why this part of the handbook exists at all. Teams rarely write these pages while things are calm. They write them after an evening that went badly, when the memory of what nobody could find is still sharp, and the page that results is usually a list of the things somebody wished had been written down. This one is no different, and you should read it in that spirit rather than as a procedure handed down from anywhere in particular.

Every migration ships with a down script, and the down script is run in staging against a restored production snapshot before the up script is allowed anywhere near production. A migration whose down script has never been executed is not reversible; it only looks reversible, which is worse, because it is the thing everyone reaches for first at two in the morning.

Reverse in the opposite order to the one you applied. If the deploy added a column, backfilled it, and then made it non-nullable, the rollback drops the constraint first, leaves the data alone, and only removes the column once the old build has been serving traffic for a full day. Dropping the column in the same step as the revert throws away the backfill, and a second attempt at the migration then has to recompute hours of work while the service is already degraded.
